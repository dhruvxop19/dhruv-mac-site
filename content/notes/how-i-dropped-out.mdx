i started in tech when i was 12.

not because i had a plan.

not because i was thinking about jobs, packages, startups, yc, or any of the things people now attach to "learning to code."

i just liked games.

so i learned game dev.

i would build because it was fun. because making a character move on a screen felt like magic. because there was something insane about having an idea in your head and turning it into something other people could actually play.

that was probably the purest version of me building.

then the indian academic machine arrived.

high school. boards. jee. engineering college. good package. stable life.

the usual roadmap.

i followed it blindly because everyone around me made it sound like the only respectable path. if you wanted a "good career," you did the same thing everyone else was doing, just with more stress and less sleep.

so i stopped building for fun and started running in the rat race.

the funny part is, i did not even know where the race ended.

i just knew everyone was running.

then i failed jee.

which, at the time, felt like the worst thing that could have happened.

in hindsight, it was probably the first crack in the script.

after jee, i got into aviation.

i spent almost a year there, and for the first time in a while, i had enough breathing room to explore again. tech slowly came back into my life. i started learning, experimenting, and remembering that i actually liked this stuff before anyone told me it had to become a career.

but some part of me still believed college was mandatory.

especially for tech.

i thought you needed the degree, the campus, the placement cell, the official stamp that said, yes, this guy is allowed to have a career now.

so i joined NIAT.

it was pitched as this new-age tech college that would teach things beyond old-school engineering. the kind of place that sounded like it understood the future.

i was convinced.

i paid the fees.

then reality hit.

for me, it felt worse than the thing it claimed to replace.

the gap between the pitch and the actual experience was brutal. i was already doing work outside college that felt more real, more useful, and more connected to the world than anything happening inside the system.

and the more i built, the more absurd the situation became.

i was getting job opportunities that were way beyond what my tier-3 college could even advertise as its highest package.

but instead of support, i got friction.

no NOC.

no real help.

no sense that the institution was there to back students who were actually trying to do something.

college was not becoming a launchpad.

it was becoming a tax on my time.

the weird thing is, i was still doing the things i loved.

i was building, learning web dev, exploring web3, hacking on ideas, and slowly finding my way back into tech.

but i was doing all of it in the gaps.

between classes.

after pointless work.

inside whatever leftover hours the system had not eaten.

then i joined X.

that changed a lot.

suddenly i could see how the real world worked.

people were building companies without waiting for permission. people were getting hired because they could ship. people were creating leverage through proof of work, distribution, taste, and speed.

the internet made college feel smaller every day.

not because learning became irrelevant.

learning became more important than ever.

but the degree started looking less like a door and more like a receipt.

i wanted to drop out.

my parents, obviously, did not.

and honestly, i get it.

from their point of view, dropping out sounded like voluntarily walking away from safety. for them, college was not just college. it was insurance. status. stability. the thing you hold on to when everything else feels uncertain.

so i tried convincing them.

it did not work at first.

then i went to bengaluru for a hackathon.

that trip did something to me.

i met builders who were living in the world i wanted to live in. people who cared about shipping, not attendance. proof, not permission. velocity, not labels.

and then i met [Divya Ranjan](https://x.com/divyaranjan_).

his story hit me hard.

not in a motivational-speaker way.

more like: oh, this is actually possible.

someone could choose the harder, less obvious path and still make it work if they were good enough, serious enough, and honest enough about what they wanted.

that rewired something.

i came back with a different kind of conviction.

for the next two months, i grinded like crazy.

i was not trying to make a dramatic point.

i was trying to build enough proof that my parents could look at the decision and see that it was not some random impulse.

i wanted them to see that college was not the thing creating my future.

the work was.

the internet was.

the people i was meeting were.

the opportunities i was creating were.

the version of me that was alive while building was.

eventually, it happened.

i dropped out.

not because college is useless for everyone.

not because dropping out is some cool founder aesthetic.

but because in my specific case, staying had become more risky than leaving.

it was slowing me down, draining my time, and keeping me attached to a version of success i did not even want anymore.

dropping out was not me quitting education.

it was me choosing the form of education that was actually working for me.

building.

shipping.

writing.

selling.

meeting people.

getting rejected.

winning sometimes.

learning in public.

learning through consequences.

that is the dropout story.

not cinematic.

not clean.

not some perfectly planned rebellion.

just a 12-year-old game-dev kid who got pulled into the rat race, escaped through the internet, found proof in the real world, and finally chose the thing he loved most.
